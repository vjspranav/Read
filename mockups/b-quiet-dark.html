A reading site for people who know the words but can't yet assemble the sentence. Warm near-black ground, one accent the colour of a lamp, and three layers of text that have to sit on top of one another without turning to mush.

Why it reads this way

  • The English is the same typeface as the French, in italic — one voice, saying the thing twice. A sans here would sound like a machine talking over the story.
  • It is set smaller and indented by 1.3em, so the ragged left edge tells you at a glance which layer you are in.
  • Hidden is not gone — it is set at 11% opacity. You can see something is there, which is the whole point of the nudge.

  • Three voices, not three sizes. French in roman, English in the same serif italic, gloss in the only sans on the page. The gloss is machinery, so it is allowed to sound mechanical.
  • The gloss is the only cool grey. Everything else — paper, ink, lamp — is warm. Hue does the separating so the layers can sit close without fighting.
  • Proximity ladder: 6px word to gloss, 2px gloss to English, 34px line to line. Grouping does the work that boxes and rules would otherwise have to do.
  • Elision keeps its shape. l’avait is two tokens with two glosses; jusqu’à is two tokens sharing one. Both get a faint tint behind them so a split word never reads as a typo.

In this sentence

Negation is two pieces that sandwich the conjugated verb. Here the verb is «avait», not «entendu» — so «ne» and «pas» wrap around «avait» and leave the past participle outside. The object pronoun «l’» squeezes in between «ne» and the verb.

Negation wraps the conjugated verb
French negates with a pair: «ne» before the verb, «pas» after it. In compound tenses the pair closes around the helping verb, not the participle. «Je n’ai pas mangé», never «Je n’ai mangé pas».
