fr  Camille travaille dans un restaurant, en ville.

fr  Ce soir-là, elle a fini très tard, après minuit.

fr  Elle a couru jusqu'à l'arrêt, mais le bus était déjà parti.
en  She ran to the stop, but the bus had already left.
?   jusqu'à | jusqua
    «à» alone would just say where she ended up. «jusqu'à» adds the whole
    distance she covered getting there — she ran the entire way.
?   était déjà parti | plus-que-parfait
    The bus leaving happened before Camille got to the stop — a past inside
    the past — so French reaches one step further back than the passé
    composé: «était parti», not «est parti».
=   à | to
=   l' | the
=   le | the
=   était | was

fr  Le dernier bus de la nuit ne repasse pas avant demain.
en  The last bus of the night doesn't come back before tomorrow.
?   ne ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    «repasse» is the only verb in this sentence, so «ne» and «pas» just wrap
    it directly — the same plain pattern as «je ne mange pas».
=   le | the
=   de | of
=   la | the
=   pas | not-2

fr  Camille n'a pas d'argent pour un taxi.
en  Camille doesn't have money for a taxi.
?   pas d'argent | de-after-negation
    Said as a plain fact this would be «de l'argent». Once «ne ... pas»
    lands in the sentence, the amount stops being named at all and the
    article shrinks to bare «d'».
=   pas | not-2
=   d' | any

fr  Alors elle décide d'attendre, même sans raison.
en  So she decides to wait, even without a reason.
=   d' | to
=   même | even

fr  Elle s'est assise sur le banc de l'arrêt.
en  She sat down on the bench at the stop.
?   s'est assise | passe-compose-etre
    «s'asseoir» is reflexive, and reflexive verbs always build their past
    with «être». Camille is «elle», so the participle picks up an «e»:
    «assise», the way an adjective would.
=   s' | herself
=   est | is
=   le | the
=   de | of
=   l' | the

fr  Il faisait froid, mais le ciel était clair.
en  It was cold, but the sky was clear.
?   faisait froid | faire-expressions
    English says "it was cold" with "to be". French reaches for «faire»
    instead — nobody makes the cold happen, but the weather still takes this
    verb, not «être».
=   il | it
=   le | the
=   était | was

fr  Elle a levé les yeux et a vu des étoiles.
en  She looked up and saw some stars.
?   les yeux | body-parts-definite-article
    They're obviously her own eyes, so French skips «ses» and uses the plain
    «les» instead. Adding a possessive here would sound like a learner
    overexplaining.
?   des étoiles | partitive-article
    Not one specific star — an unnamed number of them — so the plural
    partitive «des» marks that. English just says "stars" and leaves the
    amount unsaid.
=   les | the

fr  Un jeune homme est arrivé avec un grand sac à dos.
en  A young man arrived with a big backpack.
?   jeune | adjective-position-default
    «jeune» is one of the short, common adjectives — about age, like
    «vieux» — that jump in front of the noun instead of following it.
?   grand | adjective-position-default
    Same rule, different noun: «grand» is about size, another word on that
    short list, so it sits before «sac à dos» too.
=   est | is
=   à | on

fr  « Le bus est passé ? » a-t-il demandé.
en  "Has the bus gone by?" he asked.
?   a-t-il | euphonic-t-inversion
    Flipping «il a» to ask a question would give «a-il», two vowels running
    straight into each other. French drops in a «t» that means nothing on
    its own — it only exists to make the question sayable.
=   le | the
=   est | is
=   il | he

fr  « Oui, il y a dix minutes », a répondu Camille.
en  "Yes, ten minutes ago," Camille replied.
?   il y a | il-y-a-impersonal
    The same three words that usually mean "there is" can give a time
    instead: «il y a dix minutes» is "ten minutes ago", the same way «il y a
    trois ans» means "three years ago".
=   il | it
=   y | there

fr  Il s'appelle Léo et il rentre de la bibliothèque.
en  His name is Léo, and he's coming back from the library.
?   s'appelle | reflexive-verbs
    Word for word, «il s'appelle Léo» is "he calls himself Léo" — French
    always uses the reflexive here, where English just says "his name is".
=   il | he
=   s' | himself
=   de | from
=   la | the

fr  Il s'est assis à côté de Camille.
en  He sat down next to Camille.
?   s'est assis | passe-compose-etre
    Same reflexive verb as Camille's, but Léo is «il», not «elle», so the
    participle stays in its plain form: «assis», no extra «e».
=   il | he
=   s' | himself
=   est | is
=   à | at
=   de | of

fr  Ils attendent ensemble, sans parler.
en  They wait together, without talking.
?   sans parler | preposition-plus-infinitive
    English would say "without talking", an -ing form. After a preposition
    like «sans», French always uses the plain dictionary form of the verb
    instead: «parler».

fr  Dix minutes passent, mais le bus n'arrive toujours pas.
en  Ten minutes go by, but the bus still doesn't come.
?   n'arrive ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    One more plain example: «arrive» is the only verb, so «ne» and «pas»
    close around it directly, with «toujours» sitting inside the sandwich.
=   le | the
=   pas | not-2

fr  Un homme est arrivé avec une guitare sur le dos.
en  A man arrived with a guitar on his back.
?   sur le dos | body-parts-definite-article
    It's obviously his own back, so French uses the plain «le», not «son».
    The same move you saw with «les yeux» a moment ago works for any body
    part.
=   est | is
=   le | the

fr  Il a posé sa guitare contre le banc et s'est assis.
en  He put his guitar against the bench and sat down.
?   sa guitare | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «sa» agrees with «guitare», a feminine word — not with the man who owns
    it. A woman with the same guitar would also say «sa guitare».
=   il | he
=   sa | his
=   le | the
=   s' | himself
=   est | is

fr  « Il n'y a plus de bus ce soir, je crois », dit-il.
en  "There's no more bus tonight, I think," he said.
?   il n'y a plus | negation-other-pairs
    Swap «pas» for «plus» in the middle of «il y a» and the meaning shifts
    from "there isn't" to "there isn't anymore" — the bus existed earlier
    tonight, and now it doesn't.
?   plus de bus | de-after-negation
    Once the sentence is negative, «un bus» can't stay whole — the article
    shrinks to bare «de», the same drop you saw with «d'argent» earlier.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   plus | no-more
=   de | any

fr  Karim a un sac en papier, avec du pain dedans.
en  Karim has a paper bag, with bread inside.
?   du pain | partitive-article
    Not a whole, specific loaf — an amount with no number attached, so the
    partitive «du» carries that sense here, the same as the bread in
    another story.
=   en | made-of

fr  « Vous avez faim ? » demande-t-il à Camille et à Léo.
en  "Are you hungry?" he asks Camille and Léo.
?   avez faim | avoir-expressions
    English uses "to be" for hunger. French uses «avoir» — literally "to
    have" — plus the bare noun «faim», with no article at all.
=   il | he
=   à | to

fr  Ils partagent le pain, assis sur le banc.
en  They share the bread, sitting on the bench.
?   assis sur le banc | bare-descriptive-phrase
    No verb, no "while" — French just tacks the state on after a comma.
    English needs something like "sitting" to do the same job.
=   le | the

fr  Une femme est arrivée, très fatiguée.
en  A woman arrived, very tired.
?   fatiguée | adjective-agreement
    «une femme» is feminine, so the adjective takes the extra «e» — not
    «fatigué», which would describe a man.
=   femme | woman
=   est | is

fr  Elle s'appelle Nadia et elle travaille à l'hôpital, la nuit.
en  Her name is Nadia, and she works at the hospital, at night.
?   la nuit | definite-article-days
    A bare time word names one occasion; add «la» and it becomes a routine —
    the same trick as «le soir» for every evening. Here it means she works
    nights as a rule.
=   s' | herself
=   à | at
=   l' | the
=   la | the

=   le | the

fr  « Moi aussi, je l'ai raté », répond Léo.
en  "Me too, I missed it," replies Léo.
?   Moi aussi | stressed-pronouns
    «je» can only stand glued to a verb. Standing alone for emphasis, at the
    front of the sentence, needs the strong form instead: «moi».
?   l'ai | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «l'» stands for the bus and sits in front of «ai», the helping verb.
    English puts it after the verb — missed it — but French moves it left
    and keeps it there.
=   l' | it

fr  « Je crois qu'il ne va plus venir », dit Nadia.
en  "I think it's not going to come anymore," says Nadia.
?   qu'il | reported-speech-que
    English can drop "that" here — I think it's not coming. French can't;
    «que» has to introduce what she thinks, and it shortens to «qu'» before
    the vowel in «il».
?   ne va plus venir | negation-wraps-verb
    «va» is the conjugated piece; «venir» is the plain infinitive after it.
    The negation wraps «va» and leaves «venir» outside, the same split you'd
    see with a helping verb in the passé composé.
=   qu' | that
=   il | it
=   plus | no-more

fr  Personne ne se plaint, même après une heure de marche.
en  Nobody complains, even after an hour of walking.
?   Personne ne | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» is the subject here, so it sits at the front, before «ne».
    The pair still needs «ne», but there's no «pas» — «personne» already
    carries the negative on its own, the same way «plus» and «jamais» do
    elsewhere in this story.
=   personne | nobody
=   se | himself
=   même | even
=   de | of

fr  Ils arrivent d'abord chez Camille.
en  They arrive at Camille's place first.
?   chez Camille | chez
    «chez» has no real English equivalent — it means at the home of the
    person named after it. You can't say «chez la maison»; for a plain
    building you'd use «à» instead.

fr  Camille regarde ses amis partir, puis elle sourit.
en  Camille watches her friends leave, then she smiles.
?   ses amis | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «ses» marks a plural owned thing, not a plural owner — it stays «ses»
    whether the friends are men, women, or both, because «amis» is plural.

fr  Ce dernier bus n'est jamais passé, et personne ne le regrette.
en  That last bus never came, and nobody regrets it.
?   n'est jamais | negation-other-pairs
    «jamais» swaps in for «pas» one more time, and the meaning becomes
    "never" — the bus didn't just fail to come once tonight, it never came
    at all.
?   le regrette | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «le» stands for the bus and moves to the front of the verb group, right
    between «ne» and «regrette». English leaves the object after the verb —
    regrets it — but French always tucks the pronoun in before.
=   est | is
=   personne | nobody
=   le | it
