fr  Le vieil homme ferma la porte sans bruit.
en  The old man closed the door without a sound.
?   vieil | adjective-before-vowel
    «vieux homme» would put the «eu» sound straight against the «o» of «homme»,
    which French avoids, so the word changes shape to «vieil». Say it out loud and
    you can hear why: vieil-homme runs together, vieux-homme stumbles.
?   ferma | passe-simple-in-stories
    You'd expect «a fermé» here, and in speech that's exactly what you'd get. This
    is written storytelling, so the verb takes its written-only form, «ferma». It
    means the same thing; it just sounds like a book rather than a conversation.
=   Le | the
=   la | the

fr  Elle ne l'avait pas entendu partir.
en  She hadn't heard him leave.
?   l' | elision-before-vowel
    This «l'» is «le», meaning him — her husband. «Le avait» is unsayable, so the
    vowel drops out and the word leans onto the next one.
?   ne ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    The two halves close around «avait», leaving «entendu partir» outside on the
    right. Watch where «pas» lands: after the small verb that carries the tense,
    not after the verb that carries the meaning.
?   avait | plus-que-parfait
    «avait entendu» is a step further back than the rest of the story. He had
    already gone; her not-hearing happened before that. French marks that extra
    step back the same way English does, with «had» plus the participle.
=   l' | him
=   pas | not-2

fr  Dehors, il pleuvait encore.
en  Outside, it was still raining.
?   il | weather-il
    Nothing is doing the raining here. This «il» is a placeholder, the way English
    says "it's raining" without meaning anything by "it".
=   il | it

fr  Il marcha jusqu'à la gare, les mains dans les poches.
en  He walked to the station, his hands in his pockets.
?   jusqu'à | jusqua
    «à la gare» on its own would just say where he ended up. «jusqu'à» adds the
    whole distance — he walked the entire way, no bus, no lift.
?   les mains dans les poches | body-parts-definite-article
    English needs "his hands, his pockets" here; French says «les». They're
    obviously his hands, so marking it would be fussy, and «ses mains dans ses
    poches» sounds like someone learning the language.
=   il | he
=   à | to
=   la | the
=   les | the

fr  Le train de 7h12 partait sans lui, comme chaque mardi.
en  The 7:12 train was leaving without him, like every Tuesday.
?   partait | imparfait-vs-passe-simple
    «partait» leaves the departure open — the train pulling out, the thing that
    happens every Tuesday, the background he's standing in. Compare the last line
    of the story, where the same train «partit». That one is finished.
?   sans lui | stressed-pronouns
    After «sans» you can't use the little «le» that hides in front of a verb. The
    standalone form «lui» is what goes after a preposition, and it's the same
    word you'd use alone: «Lui ? Jamais.»
=   Le | the
=   de | of
=   lui | him

fr  Il s'était arrêté au coin de la rue, sans raison, et cela avait suffi.
en  He'd stopped at the corner for no reason, and that had been enough.
?   s'était arrêté | reflexive-verbs
    Plain «arrêter» means stopping something else — a car, a thief. To come to a
    halt yourself you stop yourself, so the verb needs its «se»: «s'arrêter». And
    once that «se» is there the past is built with «être», never «avoir».

    «Pendant» is for a stretch of time that's over and done with. The forty years

fr  Il connaissait le nom de chaque gare entre chez lui et Rouen.
en  He knew the name of every station between his house and Rouen.
?   connaissait | imparfait-vs-passe-simple
    Knowing the names wasn't an event with a start and a finish — it was simply
    true of him. States like this take «connaissait», not «connut». «Il connut» would
    mean he found out, in one moment.
?   chez lui | chez
    There's no French word for "home" as a place you go to. «chez» plus a person
    means at or to that person's place, so «chez lui» is his house, his flat,
    wherever he lives.

fr  Aujourd'hui, il n'y avait plus de bureau, plus de collègues, plus personne pour l'attendre.
en  Now there was no office, no colleagues, nobody waiting for him.
?   n'y avait plus | negation-other-pairs
    «pas» isn't the only word that finishes a negation. «plus» in this position
    means not any more — the office existed once and doesn't now. Read it aloud
    with a silent «s»; that's usually what tells it apart from «plus» meaning more.
?   plus de bureau | de-after-negation
    You'd expect «un bureau». After a negation the article collapses to a bare
    «de», so «un bureau» becomes «plus de bureau» — the same thing happens to
    «des collègues» right after it.
?   pour l'attendre | preposition-plus-infinitive
    English puts "-ing" after the preposition: nobody waiting. French can't do
    that, so «pour» is followed by the plain «attendre», with the «l'» meaning him
    tucked in front of it.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   plus | no-more
=   de | any
=   personne | nobody
=   l' | him

fr  Mais le mardi, il partait quand même.
en  But on Tuesdays he set out all the same.
?   le mardi | definite-article-days
    «mardi» on its own would mean one particular Tuesday. Adding «le» turns it
    into every Tuesday, the standing arrangement — which is the whole point of

fr  Il prenait un café en face de son ancien bureau, marchait une heure, puis rentrait.
en  He'd have a coffee across from his old office, walk for an hour, then come home.
?   prenait | imparfait-vs-passe-simple
    Three verbs in a row, all in the same form, because none of them is a single
    event. This is what he did every Tuesday, the shape of the day rather than
    one particular day. English reaches for "he'd" to say the same thing.
?   ancien bureau | adjective-position-meaning
    In front of the noun, «ancien» means former — the office he used to work in.
    Put it after and «un bureau ancien» would be a piece of antique furniture,
    which is a very different sentence.

fr  Ce matin-là, il rentra à onze heures, les chaussures trempées.
en  That morning he came home at eleven, his shoes soaked.
?   rentra | imparfait-vs-passe-simple
    Everything before this line was the routine. This one verb changes form, and
    that alone tells you the routine broke — one particular morning, one specific
    arrival, eleven o'clock instead of the evening.
?   les chaussures trempées | bare-descriptive-phrase
    French can hang a bare noun phrase on the end of a sentence to describe the
    state someone is in, with no verb and no "with". It's the same move as «les
    mains dans les poches» in the fourth line.

?   qu' | reported-speech-que
    English lets you drop "that" — he told her he'd missed it. French never
    does; «qu'» has to be there. And since «il» starts with a vowel, «que» loses
    its own vowel and becomes «qu'».

fr  Elle ne lui demanda pas lequel.
en  She didn't ask him which one.
?   lequel | lequel
    "Which one" is a single word in French, and it carries the gender and number
    of what it stands for. It's standing in for «le train» here, so it comes out
    masculine and singular: «lequel».

fr  Le vendredi suivant, il se leva avant le réveil.
en  The following Friday he got up before the alarm.
?   se leva | reflexive-verbs
    «lever» is to lift something. What he lifts is himself, so the «se» is doing
    real work: «se lever» is how French says get up. Drop it and you've said he
    lifted something, and the sentence stops making sense.

fr  Les portes se fermèrent.
en  The doors closed.
?   se fermèrent | reflexive-passive
    Nobody is closing them, and «les portes fermèrent» would be wrong. The «se»
    is how French says a thing happens to itself when the doer is irrelevant —
    here, the doors close on their own schedule, which is rather the point.

    Word for word this is "it there had", which is no help at all. Taken together

fr  Elle en mettait deux depuis mardi.
en  She'd been setting out two since Tuesday.
?   en | pronoun-en
    «en» stands in for «des tasses» — of them. French won't let you say «elle
    mettait deux» and stop there; if you don't name the thing, «en» has to hold
    its place in front of the verb.
?   depuis | duration-pendant-depuis
    «depuis» plus this form of the verb is how French says something started back
    then and is still going. English needs a whole scaffolding — she'd been
    setting out — where French needs one preposition.
