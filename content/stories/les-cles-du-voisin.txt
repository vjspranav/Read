summary: A narrator finds a stranger's keys on the stairs, spends a week trying to return them, and finally meets the neighbour they had already crossed paths with many times without knowing their name.

fr  Un lundi matin, il faisait encore froid, et l'escalier de mon immeuble était vide.
en  One Monday morning, it was still cold, and the stairway of my building was empty.
?   il | weather-il
    «il» doesn't stand for a person or a thing here — French just needs a
    subject for weather, the same empty «il» you'd see in «il pleut», it's
    raining. Nothing is actually cold in a way «il» could point to.
?   l' | elision-before-vowel
    «le» would run straight into «escalier», a word starting with a vowel
    sound, so it drops its own vowel and becomes «l'». You'll see the same
    move happen to «de» later in the story.
=   il | it
=   l' | the
=   de | of
=   était | was

fr  J'ai trouvé un porte-clés sur une marche, tout seul.
en  I found a keyring on a step, all alone.

fr  Il y avait deux clés dessus, une grande et une petite.
en  There were two keys on it, a big one and a small one.
?   Il y avait | il-y-a-impersonal
    «il», «y», and «avait» only mean something as one block: there was. It's
    how the keys get introduced into the story, the same way «il y a» would
    do it in the present.
?   grande | adjective-agreement
    «clé» is feminine, so both size words end in «e» to match it — «grand»
    and «petit» would be the versions for a masculine noun. You can't hear
    the difference out loud, but it has to be written.
=   il | it
=   y | there

fr  Elles n'étaient pas à moi.
en  They weren't mine.
?   à moi | etre-a-possession
    French doesn't have a separate verb for belonging — «être» plus «à» plus
    a person does the job. «Elles sont à moi» would mean they're mine; the
    negation here just flips it around.
?   n' ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    Only one verb here, «étaient», so «ne» and «pas» simply close around it —
    no helping verb to complicate things yet, unlike the compound tenses
    coming up soon.
=   étaient | were
=   pas | not-2
=   à | to

fr  Je me suis dit que quelqu'un a perdu ses clés.
en  I told myself that someone had lost their keys.
?   me suis dit | reflexive-verbs
    «se dire» means to tell yourself something — «me» shows the telling
    loops back to the narrator. Reflexive verbs like this always build the
    passé composé with «être», never «avoir», which is why it's «suis», not
    «ai».
?   que | reported-speech-que
    English could drop that here — I told myself someone lost their keys —
    but French can't. «que» has to introduce what was said, even inside your
    own head.

fr  Je voulais savoir à qui étaient ces clés.
en  I wanted to know whose keys these were.
?   à qui | etre-a-possession
    Asking whose something is uses the same «être à» pattern as saying it
    plainly, just turned into a question. «à qui» stands where «à moi» or
    «à elle» would go, asking which person the «à» points to.
=   à | to
=   étaient | were

fr  Au premier étage, j'ai frappé à la porte d'un vieil homme.
en  On the first floor, I knocked on an old man's door.
?   vieil | adjective-before-vowel
    «vieux» would put two vowel sounds back to back in «vieux homme» — the
    «h» is silent, so it's really «vieux + omme». French swaps in the
    special form «vieil» to avoid that collision.
=   à | at
=   la | the
=   d' | of

fr  Ce n'était pas ses clés non plus.
en  They weren't his keys either.
=   était | was
=   pas | not-2
=   plus | either

fr  Au deuxième étage, une dame m'a dit que ces clés n'étaient pas à elle.
en  On the second floor, a lady told me these keys weren't hers.
?   que | reported-speech-que
    Same rule as before: what she said has to be introduced with «que», even
    though English would happily just say a lady told me these keys weren't
    hers.
?   à elle | etre-a-possession
    Same shape as «à moi» a few lines back, just with «elle» instead — the
    standalone pronoun that has to follow a preposition like «à».
=   étaient | were
=   pas | not-2
=   à | to

fr  Au troisième étage, j'ai frappé, mais personne n'a répondu.
en  On the third floor, I knocked, but nobody answered.
?   personne n'a répondu | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» takes the place of «pas» here and means nobody — it's the
    subject of the sentence, not something sitting after the verb the way it
    does in «il n'y a personne». It still pairs with «ne» the same way
    «plus» and «jamais» do.
=   personne | nobody

fr  Je suis descendu à la boulangerie, en bas de l'immeuble.
en  I went down to the bakery, at the bottom of the building.
?   suis descendu | passe-compose-etre
    «descendre» is one of the small set of movement verbs that build the
    passé composé with «être» instead of «avoir» — the same family as
    «aller» and «rentrer». That's why it's «je suis descendu», never «j'ai
    descendu».
=   à | at
=   la | the
=   en | in
=   de | of
=   l' | the

fr  J'ai montré les clés au boulanger, mais il ne connaissait pas ces clés.
en  I showed the keys to the baker, but he didn't know them.
?   il ne connaissait pas | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    «j'ai montré» is the event — one action, holding the keys out. «il ne
    connaissait pas» is imparfait because not recognising them isn't
    something that happened; it's the state the baker was already in.
=   il | he
=   les | the
=   pas | not-2

fr  Alors, j'ai écrit un mot et je l'ai collé près des boîtes aux lettres.
en  So, I wrote a note and stuck it up near the mailboxes.
?   je l'ai collé | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «l'» stands for the note and sits right in front of «ai», the helper
    verb. English says stuck it up after the verb; French moves the pronoun
    left and keeps it there.
=   l' | it

fr  Le mot disait : « J'ai trouvé des clés. Elles ne sont pas à moi. Elles sont peut-être à vous ? »
en  The note said: "I found some keys. They aren't mine. Maybe they're yours?"
?   des clés | partitive-article
    «clés» is plural, so «des» does the job «du» or «de la» would do for
    something singular — an unspecified number of keys, not a count.
?   à moi | etre-a-possession
    Both halves of the note lean on the same «être à» pattern the narrator
    used out loud earlier — «à moi» rules the keys out as the narrator's,
    and «à vous» asks whoever reads the note if they're theirs.
=   le | the
=   pas | not-2
=   à | to

fr  J'ai laissé mon numéro de téléphone au bas de la page.
en  I left my phone number at the bottom of the page.
=   de | of
=   la | the

fr  Un voisin a lu mon mot, et il m'a dit : « C'est gentil, ça. »
en  A neighbour read my note, and told me: "That's kind of you."
=   il | he
=   est | is

fr  Mardi, personne n'a téléphoné.
en  On Tuesday, nobody phoned.
=   personne | nobody

fr  Mercredi, j'ai demandé à la gardienne, mais elle ne savait rien.
en  On Wednesday, I asked the caretaker, but she didn't know anything.
?   elle ne savait rien | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    «j'ai demandé» is the one-off event, going and asking. «elle ne savait
    rien» is imparfait because not knowing isn't an action — it's a state
    she was already in before the narrator even asked.
=   à | at
=   la | the

fr  J'ai laissé les clés sur la table de la cuisine.
en  I left the keys on the kitchen table.
=   les | the
=   la | the
=   de | of

fr  Chaque soir, en rentrant, je les voyais sur la table.
en  Every evening, coming home, I saw them on the table.
?   en rentrant | preposition-plus-infinitive
    «en» is the one preposition that doesn't take the plain infinitive after
    it — it takes this «-ant» form instead. «En rentrant» means while coming
    home, something happening at the same time as noticing the keys.
?   je les voyais | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «les» stands for the keys and sits in front of «voyais» rather than
    after it — the same leftward move object pronouns always make.
=   la | the
=   en | while
=   les | them

fr  Les clés n'étaient plus seulement des clés.
en  The keys weren't just keys any more.
?   n' ... plus | negation-other-pairs
    «plus» here doesn't mean more — paired with «ne», it means not any more.
    The keys used to be just keys, and that had stopped being true.
=   les | the
=   étaient | were
=   plus | no-more

fr  Elles sont devenues un petit poids sur la table.
en  They became a small weight on the table.
?   sont devenues | passe-compose-etre
    «devenir» is another être verb, like «descendre» earlier — and because
    the subject is «elles», the keys, the participle picks up an «s»,
    «devenues», agreeing the way an adjective would.
=   la | the

fr  Jeudi, j'ai posé une tasse de café à côté des clés, mais personne n'est venu.
en  Thursday, I put a cup of coffee next to the keys, but nobody came.
?   personne n'est venu | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» is the subject again, nobody, and «venir» is one of the être
    verbs — so together it's «personne n'est venu». «venu» stays in its
    plain masculine form because «personne» is always treated as masculine,
    whoever it turns out to mean.
=   de | of
=   à | at
=   personne | nobody
=   est | is

fr  Vendredi, il y avait aussi mon courrier et un livre autour d'elles.
en  Friday, my mail and a book were around them too.
?   d'elles | elision-before-vowel
    «de» meets «elles», a vowel start, and drops its own vowel the same way
    it did with «l'escalier» right at the start of the story.
?   autour d'elles | stressed-pronouns
    After a preposition like «autour de», French needs this standalone
    «elles», not a form that hides in front of a verb — the same slot «moi»
    and «elle» filled after «à» earlier on.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   d' | of

fr  À cause des clés, ma table de cuisine était pleine de petites choses.
en  Because of the keys, my kitchen table was full of little things.
?   pleine | adjective-agreement
    «table» is feminine, so «plein» picks up an «e» to match it, «pleine».
    Say «ma table était plein» out loud and it's immediately wrong, even
    though you can't hear the missing letter.
?   petites choses | adjective-position-default
    «petit» is one of a short list of common adjectives — about size, age,
    beauty, or goodness — that jump in front of the noun instead of
    following it, unlike most French adjectives.
=   à | at
=   de | of
=   était | was

fr  Le soir, avant de dormir, je pensais encore aux clés.
en  In the evening, before sleeping, I still thought about the keys.
?   avant de dormir | preposition-plus-infinitive
    Where English puts an -ing form after before — before sleeping — French
    uses the plain dictionary form of the verb instead: «avant de dormir»,
    never «avant de dormant».
=   le | the
=   de | of

fr  Les clés étaient sur ma table depuis une semaine.
en  The keys had been on my table for a week.
?   depuis une semaine | duration-pendant-depuis
    The keys didn't sit there for a week and then stop — they were still
    there, so French uses «depuis» with the imparfait rather than «pendant»,
    which would mark a stretch that's already over.
=   les | the
=   étaient | were

fr  Je n'avais toujours pas de réponse à mon mot.
en  I still had no answer to my note.
?   pas de réponse | de-after-negation
    «réponse» would normally take «une» in front of it, an answer. Once the
    sentence is negative, there isn't one at all, so the article shrinks to
    bare «de».
=   pas | not-2
=   de | any
=   à | to

fr  Je pensais que personne n'allait venir.
en  I thought nobody was going to come.
?   personne n'allait venir | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» is the subject again — nobody was going to come — paired
    with «ne» the same way as before. This time it wraps around «allait»,
    the conjugated piece of «allait venir», while «venir» itself sits
    untouched right after it.
?   que | reported-speech-que
    «que» has to introduce the thought here too — French never drops it the
    way English drops that in I thought nobody was going to come.
=   personne | nobody

fr  Le vendredi suivant, quelqu'un a frappé à ma porte.
en  The following Friday, someone knocked at my door.
=   le | the
=   à | at

fr  J'ai ouvert la porte.
en  I opened the door.
=   la | the

fr  C'était la femme de la boulangerie.
en  It was the woman from the bakery.
?   C'était | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    «j'ai ouvert» just before this is the event, one action, opening the
    door. «c'était» is imparfait because it isn't something that happened —
    it's simply who turned out to already be standing there.
=   était | was
=   la | the
=   femme | woman
=   de | of

fr  Je la voyais presque tous les matins, mais je ne connaissais pas son nom.
en  I saw her almost every morning, but I didn't know her name.
?   je la voyais | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «la» stands for the woman and sits in front of «voyais», the same
    leftward move «les» made earlier when it stood for the keys.
?   son nom | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «son» agrees with «nom», a masculine word — it says nothing about the
    woman herself, even though «son» looks like it should mean his. If the
    word for name were feminine, this same sentence about this same woman
    would use «sa» instead.
=   la | her
=   les | the
=   pas | not-2
=   son | his

fr  Elle achetait toujours la même chose, un café et un croissant.
en  She always bought the same thing, a coffee and a croissant.
=   la | the
=   même | same

fr  « Excusez-moi, a-t-elle dit. Je crois que ce sont mes clés. »
en  "Excuse me," she said. "I think these are my keys."
?   a-t-elle dit | euphonic-t-inversion
    Dropping in a dialogue tag with «elle a» would collide two vowel
    sounds, «a» and «elle». The «t» that slides between them doesn't mean
    anything on its own — it's only there to make the words sayable
    together.
?   que | reported-speech-que
    «que» is required here too — French can't just say I think these are my
    keys without it, the way English sometimes drops that.

fr  « J'ai cherché ces clés partout », a-t-elle expliqué.
en  "I've looked for these keys everywhere," she explained.

fr  Je lui ai donné les clés tout de suite.
en  I gave her the keys right away.
?   lui ai donné | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «lui» stands for the woman, to her, and sits in front of «ai» instead of
    after «donné» the way English puts it. It works whether the person is
    male or female; only the sentence tells you which.
=   lui | her
=   les | the
=   de | of

fr  Elle m'a remercié, et puis elle a souri.
en  She thanked me, and then she smiled.

fr  Elle m'a dit qu'elle s'appelle Camille.
en  She told me her name was Camille.
?   qu' | reported-speech-que
    «que» is doing the same job again, introducing what she said, and
    shortens to «qu'» because «elle» starts with a vowel.
?   s'appelle | reflexive-verbs
    «s'appeler» is how French says to be called — literally to call
    yourself. It stays in the present here even though everything around it
    is passé composé, because her name being Camille isn't a one-off event;
    it's a fact that's still true.
=   qu' | that
=   s' | herself

fr  Moi, je lui ai dit mon nom aussi.
en  I told her my name too.
?   Moi | stressed-pronouns
    «Moi» standing alone at the front adds emphasis, as for me — something
    «je» alone couldn't do. It's the standalone form French uses whenever a
    pronoun needs to carry weight on its own, not glued to a verb.
=   lui | her

fr  Nous avons ri, parce que nous habitions dans le même immeuble depuis longtemps.
en  We laughed, because we had been living in the same building for a long time.
?   habitions | duration-pendant-depuis
    «habitions» is imparfait because living in the same building wasn't a
    single event — paired with «depuis longtemps» at the end of the
    sentence, it says the living-there had already been true for a while by
    the time they met, exactly the kind of ongoing fact «depuis» pairs with.
=   le | the
=   même | same

fr  Depuis ce jour, Camille et moi, nous prenons un café ensemble le samedi.
en  Since that day, Camille and I have coffee together on Saturdays.
?   Depuis ce jour | duration-pendant-depuis
    This time «depuis» pairs with the present tense, «prenons» at the end of
    the sentence, because the coffee habit hasn't stopped — it's still
    going as the narrator tells the story. Compare «depuis longtemps» a
    moment ago, which used the imparfait for a fact about the past instead.
?   le samedi | definite-article-days
    A bare «samedi» would mean one particular Saturday. Adding «le» turns it
    into a routine, every Saturday, the same trick that turned «vendredi»
    into a habit here after a whole week of specific, one-off days earlier
    in the story.
=   le | the

fr  Sur ma table, il n'y a plus de clés — seulement deux tasses vides.
en  On my table, there are no more keys — just two empty cups.
?   il n'y a plus | il-y-a-impersonal
    «il y a», here negated to «il n'y a plus», is the same fixed block from
    earlier in the story, now saying there's nothing left instead of
    announcing what's there.
?   vides | adjective-agreement
    «tasses» is feminine and plural, so «vide» picks up an «s» for the
    plural — it already ends in «e», so no separate feminine ending is
    needed on top of it.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   plus | no-more
=   de | any
