fr  Chaque samedi, ma mère écrit une liste pour les courses.
en  Every Saturday, my mother writes a list for the shopping.
=   les | the

fr  Son écriture est un vrai désastre.
en  Her handwriting is a real disaster.
=   son | her
=   est | is

fr  Personne ne la comprend bien.
en  Nobody understands it well.
?   Personne ne | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» is doing the job of the subject here — nobody — not sitting
    after the verb the way it does in «il n'y a personne». It still pairs
    with «ne» the same way «plus» and «jamais» do; the two words just wrap
    around «la comprend» instead of following it.
=   personne | nobody
=   la | it

fr  Ce matin, elle m'a donné la liste et un peu d'argent.
en  This morning, she gave me the list and a little money.
?   m'a | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «m'» stands for "me" and sits right in front of «a», the helper verb.
    English says "gave me" with "me" after the verb; French moves it left
    and glues it there instead.
?   un peu d'argent | quantity-de
    «un peu» already says how much — a little — so French doesn't reach for
    a partitive article on top of it. It drops straight to bare «de», and
    you'll see the same move with «beaucoup de» in a moment.
?   d' | elision-before-vowel
    «de» runs into «argent», a word starting with a vowel, so it drops its
    own vowel and becomes «d'». Saying «de argent» out loud is impossible,
    which is exactly why the contraction exists.
=   la | the
=   d' | of

fr  Il n'y a plus de lait ni de pain à la maison.
en  There's no more milk or bread in the house.
?   Il n'y a | il-y-a-impersonal
    «il», «y», and «a» only mean something together — there is. It's the
    same fixed block that names what's missing from the house before the
    narrator even leaves for the shop.
?   n' ... plus | negation-other-pairs
    «plus» here doesn't mean "more" — paired with «ne», it means "not any
    more". The house hasn't got a little milk left, it's completely out.
?   de lait | de-after-negation
    «lait» would normally take «du» in front of it — some milk. Once the
    sentence is negative, there isn't any, so the article shrinks to bare
    «de». The same thing happens to «pain» a few words later.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   plus | no-more
=   de | any
=   à | at
=   la | the

fr  Je suis allé au magasin au coin de la rue.
en  I went to the shop on the corner of the street.
?   suis allé | passe-compose-etre
    «aller» is one of the small set of verbs — mostly about movement —
    that build the passé composé with «être» instead of «avoir». That's
    why it's «je suis allé», never «j'ai allé».
=   de | of
=   la | the

fr  Il y avait beaucoup de monde devant les rayons.
en  There were a lot of people in front of the shelves.
?   Il y avait | il-y-a-impersonal
    Same fixed phrase as a moment ago, just in the imparfait now — «il y
    avait» paints the scene the narrator walked into, the crowd already
    there before anything else happened.
?   beaucoup de | quantity-de
    Same move as «un peu d'argent» earlier: «beaucoup» already names the
    amount, so «monde» gets bare «de» in front of it instead of a
    partitive article.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   de | of
=   les | the

fr  Sur la liste, j'ai lu « du pain », « des œufs » et « du beurre ».
en  On the list, I read "bread", "eggs" and "butter".
?   du | partitive-article | 1
    «pain» is masculine, so «de» plus «le» contracts to «du» — some bread,
    an amount with no number attached, not one whole specific loaf.
?   des | partitive-article
    Same idea for the eggs, but plural: an unspecified number of them, so
    the plural partitive «des» does the job instead.
?   du | partitive-article | 2
    Same contraction one more time — «beurre» is masculine too, so it's
    «du» again, not «un beurre» or bare «beurre».
=   la | the

fr  J'ai aussi lu « des poireaux », je crois.
en  I also read "leeks", I think.

fr  Mais avec cette écriture, « poireaux » ressemble à « poires ».
en  But with that handwriting, "poireaux" looks like "poires".
=   à | to

fr  Alors je n'ai pas acheté de poireaux, mais des poires.
en  So I didn't buy leeks, but pears.
?   n' ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    «ai» is the helper carrying the tense, so that's what «ne» and «pas»
    wrap around, not «acheté». «Je n'ai acheté pas» is never correct.
?   de poireaux | de-after-negation
    «poireaux» would normally take «des» — some leeks. Because the verb is
    negated, the article drops to bare «de». Notice «des poires» right
    after keeps its full form: nothing is negated there, he really did buy
    them.
=   pas | not-2
=   de | any

fr  Ensuite, j'ai vu « du sel », ou peut-être « du miel ».
en  Then, I saw "salt", or maybe "honey".

fr  J'ai pris le pot le plus proche : c'était du miel.
en  I took the closest jar: it was honey.
?   c'était | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    «j'ai pris» is the event — one action, grabbing a jar. «c'était» is
    imparfait because it isn't something that happened; it's what turned
    out to already be true. The jar had been honey the whole time, he
    just hadn't looked yet.
=   le | the
=   plus | most
=   était | was

fr  Enfin, la liste disait « de la confiture », mais j'ai lu « de la moutarde ».
en  Finally, the list said "jam", but I read "mustard".
?   de la | partitive-article | 1
    «confiture» is feminine, so unlike «du pain» earlier, «de» and «la»
    don't contract — they just sit side by side as two separate words,
    both needed.
?   de la | partitive-article | 2
    Same feminine partitive, same shape, just the wrong jar this time.
=   de | of
=   la | the

fr  Je suis rentré avec un sac plein de mauvaises surprises.
en  I came home with a bag full of bad surprises.
?   suis rentré | passe-compose-etre
    «rentrer» is another of the être verbs, like «aller» earlier — coming
    home counts as the kind of movement that takes «être» instead of
    «avoir».
?   mauvaises | adjective-agreement
    «surprises» is feminine and plural, so the adjective picks up both
    endings, «-e» for feminine and «-s» for plural, even though you can't
    hear the difference when you say it.
=   de | of

fr  Devant la porte, j'ai eu peur de sa réaction.
en  In front of the door, I was afraid of her reaction.
?   sa réaction | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «sa» agrees with «réaction», a feminine word — it says nothing about
    the mother, whose reaction it is. If «réaction» were masculine, this
    same sentence about the same mother would use «son» instead.
=   la | the
=   sa | her
=   de | of

fr  Mais quand elle a vu les poires et le miel, ma mère a éclaté de rire.
en  But when she saw the pears and the honey, my mother burst out laughing.
=   les | the
=   le | the
=   de | of

fr  « Ce n'est pas grave, a-t-elle dit. On va improviser. »
en  "It's not a big deal," she said. "We'll improvise."
?   a-t-elle | euphonic-t-inversion
    Interrupting the sentence with «elle a» to add a dialogue tag would
    collide two vowel sounds, «a» and «elle». The «t» that slides between
    them doesn't mean anything — it's only there to make the words sayable
    together.
?   n' ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    Only one verb here, «est» — no helper, no participle — so «ne» and
    «pas» just close around it directly. It's the plain version of the
    same sandwich seen earlier with compound tenses.
=   pas | not-2
=   est | is

fr  Elle a coupé les poires avec un peu de fromage.
en  She cut the pears with a bit of cheese.
?   un peu de | quantity-de
    Third time this pattern shows up: «un peu» already names the amount,
    so «fromage» gets bare «de» in front of it, not a partitive article.
=   les | the
=   de | of

fr  Avec le miel, elle a préparé une sauce pour le poulet.

fr  On a mangé ce dîner bizarre, tous les deux, en riant.
en  We ate that strange dinner together, laughing.
?   en riant | preposition-plus-infinitive
    «en» is the one preposition that doesn't take the plain infinitive
    afterward — it takes this «-ant» form instead. «En riant» means "while
    laughing", the thing they were doing at the same time as eating.
=   les | the
=   en | while
