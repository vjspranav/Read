fr  Claire s'assied toujours au même bout du banc, avec un livre.
en  Claire always sits at the same end of the bench, with a book.
?   s'assied | reflexive-verbs
    «asseoir» would mean to sit someone else down. Add «s'» and it turns back
    on Claire herself — she sits herself down, which is just «to sit» in
    English.
=   s' | herself
=   même | same

fr  Un vieil homme est assis à l'autre bout, avec un sac de pain.
en  An old man sits at the other end, with a bag of bread.
?   vieil | adjective-before-vowel
    «vieux homme» would crash two vowel sounds together, so French swaps in
    the special form «vieil» right before a word starting with a vowel or a
    silent «h».
=   est | is
=   à | at
=   l' | the
=   de | of

fr  Chaque matin, il jette des morceaux de pain aux oiseaux.
en  Every morning, he throws pieces of bread to the birds.
?   des morceaux de pain | partitive-article
    He isn't throwing one whole loaf, just an unspecified number of pieces —
    «des» marks that vague amount, the same job it does with croissants or
    bread elsewhere in these stories.
=   il | he
=   de | of

fr  Claire ne lui parle jamais, et il ne la regarde jamais vraiment.
en  Claire never speaks to him, and he never really looks at her.
?   ne ... jamais | negation-other-pairs | 1
    Swap «pas» for «jamais» and the sentence goes from "doesn't" to "never" —
    the same wrapping position around the verb, just a different word for
    the negative.
=   lui | him
=   il | he
=   la | her

fr  Mais elle remarque toujours ses vieilles chaussures, usées mais propres.
en  But she always notices his old shoes, worn but clean.
?   ses | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «ses» tells you the shoes are plural and says nothing about whether the
    owner is a man or a woman. Only the story tells you it's the old man's
    shoes, not Claire's.
?   usées | adjective-agreement
    «chaussures» is feminine and plural, so the adjective picks up both
    markers at once: «usée» becomes «usées».
=   ses | his

fr  Lui, il remarque qu'elle rit parfois toute seule, en lisant.
en  He notices that she sometimes laughs alone, while reading.
?   Lui | stressed-pronouns
    «il» alone would already mean he. Putting «Lui» in front adds emphasis —
    it's his turn to notice something, after the last two lines were about
    what Claire sees.
?   qu' | reported-speech-que
    English could drop "that" here: he notices she laughs. French can't —
    «que» has to introduce what he notices, and it shortens to «qu'» before
    the vowel in «elle».
?   en lisant | preposition-plus-infinitive
    After most prepositions French uses the plain infinitive, but «en» is
    the one exception — it takes this «-ant» form instead. «en lisant» means
    while reading.
=   Lui | he
=   il | he
=   qu' | that
=   en | while

fr  Un jeudi matin, Claire arrive et s'assied comme toujours.
en  One Thursday morning, Claire arrives and sits down as always.
=   s' | herself

fr  D'habitude, l'homme était déjà là avec ses oiseaux.
en  Usually, the man was already there with his birds.
?   était | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    This sentence sets the scene: how things usually were, morning after
    morning. The imparfait paints that backdrop. The next line breaks it with
    a passé composé, for the one morning that didn't match.
=   d' | of
=   l' | the
=   était | was
=   ses | his

fr  Mais ce jour-là, le banc est resté vide à côté d'elle.
en  But that day, the bench stayed empty next to her.
?   est resté | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    «était» in the line before described the usual picture. This verb is
    passé composé because it marks the one day the picture changed — an
    empty bench is the new fact, not the routine.
?   est resté | passe-compose-etre
    «rester» is one of the small set of verbs that build their past with
    «être» instead of «avoir». The subject, «le banc», is masculine and
    singular, so the participle stays plain «resté», with no extra ending.
=   le | the
=   est | is
=   à | at
=   d' | of

fr  Elle a attendu, mais personne n'est venu nourrir les oiseaux.
en  She waited, but no one came to feed the birds.
?   personne | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» is its own negative word, meaning nobody. It doesn't team up
    with «pas» — the «ne» is enough on its own once «personne» is there.
=   personne | nobody
=   est | is
=   les | the

fr  Claire s'est rendu compte qu'elle ne connaissait même pas son nom.
en  Claire realized she didn't even know his name.
?   s'est rendu compte | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    Realizing is one sharp moment, so it's passé composé. Not knowing his
    name was already true before that moment and stays true after — that's
    why the next verb, «connaissait», switches to imparfait.
?   même pas | negation-wraps-verb
    The «ne ... pas» pair still wraps the conjugated verb, «connaissait».
    «même», meaning even, just slides in between «ne» and the verb without
    breaking the pair apart.
=   s' | herself
=   est | is
=   qu' | that
=   même | even
=   pas | not-2
=   son | his

fr  Elle ne savait pas où il habitait, ni comment le retrouver.
en  She didn't know where he lived, or how to find him.
?   le retrouver | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «le» stands for the old man and sits right in front of the verb it
    belongs to, «retrouver». English puts "him" after "find" — French always
    puts the pronoun first.
=   pas | not-2
=   il | he
=   le | him

fr  Pendant une semaine, personne n'a nourri les oiseaux à sa place.
en  For a week, no one fed the birds in his place.
?   pendant | duration-pendant-depuis
    A week is a finished stretch of time here — it started and it ended —
    so French uses «pendant». «depuis» would be wrong; that word is for
    something still going on.
=   personne | nobody
=   les | the
=   à | in
=   sa | his

fr  Le vendredi suivant, Claire a apporté du pain, juste au cas où.
en  The following Friday, Claire brought bread, just in case.
?   du | partitive-article
    Claire isn't bringing one whole, countable loaf — «pain» is masculine,
    so «de» plus «le» contracts to «du», marking an unspecified amount of
    bread.
=   le | the

fr  Et ce matin-là, l'homme est enfin revenu, plus lentement qu'avant.
en  And that morning, the man finally came back, more slowly than before.
?   est enfin revenu | passe-compose-etre
    «revenir» is built on «venir», one of the movement verbs that take
    «être» instead of «avoir» in the past. The subject «l'homme» is
    masculine and singular, so the participle needs no extra ending.
=   l' | the
=   est | is
=   plus | more
=   qu' | than

fr  Il s'est assis, son sac de pain à la main.
en  He sat down, his bag of bread in hand.
?   son sac de pain à la main | bare-descriptive-phrase
    No verb, no "with" — French just tacks the noun phrase on after a comma
    to describe how he was sitting. English needs "with his bag in hand";
    French needs nothing but the comma.
=   il | he
=   s' | himself
=   est | is
=   son | his
=   de | of
=   à | in
=   la | the

fr  Claire lui a demandé s'il allait bien.
en  Claire asked him if he was doing okay.
?   s'il | elision-si-il
    «si», meaning if, keeps its vowel almost everywhere — except right
    before «il» and «ils», where it shortens to «s'». It looks exactly like
    the «s'» of a reflexive verb but here means something completely
    different.
=   lui | him
=   s' | if
=   il | he

fr  « J'étais à l'hôpital, dit-il. Ma fille a eu un bébé. »
en  "I was at the hospital," he said. "My daughter had a baby."
?   J'étais | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    Being at the hospital is the state he's explaining, so it's imparfait.
    The next sentence, «a eu», is passé composé because the birth itself was
    one event, with a beginning and an end.
=   à | at
=   l' | the

fr  Claire a souri et lui a donné un morceau de son pain.
en  Claire smiled and gave him a piece of her bread.
?   lui a donné | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «lui» stands for the old man and moves in front of the helping verb,
    «a». English says gave him; French never puts the pronoun after
    «donné».
?   son pain | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    Two lines ago «son sac» was the old man's bag. Here «son pain» is
    Claire's bread — «son» only tells you «pain» is masculine, not who owns
    it.
=   lui | him
=   de | of
=   son | her

fr  « Merci pour le pain », a-t-il dit doucement.
en  "Thank you for the bread," he said quietly.
?   a-t-il | euphonic-t-inversion
    Flipping «il a» round for this aside would give «a-il», two vowels
    colliding. French drops in a «t» with no meaning of its own, just to
    make the inversion sayable.
=   le | the
=   il | he

fr  Maintenant, ils se parlent un peu, chaque matin, chacun à son bout du banc.
en  Now, they talk to each other a little, every morning, each at their own end of the bench.
?   se parlent | reflexive-verbs
    With «ils» as the subject, «se parler» doesn't mean each one talking to
    himself — it means talking to each other. The same «se» that turns a
    verb back on one person can also point two people at each other.
?   son | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «son» here just agrees with «bout», a masculine word, and points back at
    «chacun» — each one, whether that's Claire or the man. It doesn't pick a
    gender the way "his" or "her" would in English.
=   se | each-other
=   à | at
=   son | his
