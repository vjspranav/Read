fr  Un vieux vélo jaune apparaît devant l'immeuble, un lundi matin.
en  An old yellow bicycle appears in front of the building, one Monday morning.
?   l' | elision-before-vowel
    «immeuble» starts with a vowel sound, so «le» can't stay whole in front of
    it — it drops the «e» and becomes «l'». Say "le immeuble" out loud and
    you'll hear the clash it avoids.
?   vieux vélo jaune | adjective-position-default
    «vieux» describes age, one of the short, common adjectives that jump in
    front of the noun instead of following it. «jaune», a colour, stays in its
    normal spot after the noun, so the bicycle ends up sandwiched between the
    two.
=   l' | the

fr  C'est une vieille bicyclette, attachée à un arbre avec une chaîne, mais personne ne la reconnaît.
en  It's an old bicycle, chained to a tree with a chain, but nobody recognizes it.
?   vieille | adjective-agreement
    The bicycle just got a new name, «bicyclette», and it's feminine, so
    «vieux» has to switch to its feminine form «vieille» — same word as the
    bike in the line above, just wearing the ending that matches its new noun.
?   personne ne | negation-other-pairs
    «personne» sits before «ne» here because it's the subject of the
    sentence — nobody is the one not recognizing the bike. The negation still
    needs both pieces even though «personne» already carries the "no one"
    idea on its own.
?   la | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «la» stands for the bicycle — «bicyclette» is feminine, which is why it's
    «la» and not «le» — and it sits right in front of «reconnaît» instead of
    after it, the way English would say "recognizes it."
=   est | is
=   à | to
=   personne | nobody
=   la | it

fr  Le mardi matin, il n'est plus là.
en  On Tuesday morning, it's no longer there.
?   n'est plus | negation-other-pairs
    Swap «pas» for «plus» and the sentence stops meaning "isn't there" and
    starts meaning "isn't there anymore." The bike hasn't just vanished for a
    moment — it's genuinely gone from that spot now.
=   le | the
=   il | it
=   est | is
=   plus | no-more

fr  Quelqu'un le voit plus loin dans la rue, devant la boulangerie.
en  Someone sees it further down the street, in front of the bakery.
?   le | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «le» stands for the bicycle and sits in front of «voit» — French never
    says «quelqu'un voit le», it moves the pronoun to the left of the verb
    every time.
=   le | it
=   plus | more
=   la | the

fr  Les voisins boivent du café et inventent des théories.
en  The neighbours drink coffee and invent theories.
?   du | partitive-article
    The neighbours aren't drinking one specific, whole coffee — «café» is
    masculine, so «de» plus «le» contracts to «du», meaning some coffee, an
    amount with no number attached.
?   des | partitive-article
    Same idea for the theories: an unspecified number of them, invented on
    the spot, so the plural partitive «des» carries that sense. English just
    says theories and leaves the amount implied.
=   les | the

fr  Madame Dubois est certaine que c'est le vélo d'un voleur.
en  Madame Dubois is certain it's a thief's bicycle.
?   certaine | adjective-agreement
    Madame Dubois is a woman, so the adjective «certain» takes an «e» to
    match her: «certaine». She's the first of three confident neighbours in a
    row, and, like the others, she's wrong.
?   d' | elision-before-vowel
    «de» meets «un», another vowel sound, and contracts the same way «le» did
    to «l'» in the first line — French won't let two vowel sounds sit next to
    each other unglued.
=   est | is
=   le | the
=   d' | of

fr  Monsieur Roux est sûr que le vélo se déplace tout seul, la nuit.
en  Monsieur Roux is sure the bicycle moves by itself at night.
?   se déplace | reflexive-passive
    Nobody is named as the one moving the bicycle — it just «se déplace»,
    moves itself. That's exactly the theory Monsieur Roux is proposing, and
    the grammar itself sounds a little spooky because of it.
?   sûr | adjective-agreement
    Monsieur Roux is a man, so «sûr» stays in its plain masculine form — no
    extra letter needed, unlike Madame Dubois's «certaine» just before.
=   est | is
=   le | the
=   se | itself
=   la | the

fr  Les enfants sont sûrs que c'est un vélo magique.
en  The kids are sure it's a magic bicycle.
=   les | the
=   est | is

fr  Chaque jour, le vélo change de rue, mais toujours avec la même roue cassée.
en  Every day, the bicycle changes streets, but always with the same broken wheel.
?   change de rue | verb-plus-de-fixed
    «changer de rue» doesn't mean "change of the street" — «de» is glued to
    «changer» as part of the verb itself, with no article ever following it.
    Word for word it looks odd; as a fixed pair it just means "switch
    streets."
?   même roue | adjective-position-meaning
    «même» in front of the noun means the same wheel, the very thing everyone
    keeps noticing. Move it after the noun — «la roue même» — and it would
    mean the wheel itself, a different idea entirely.
=   le | the
=   de | of
=   la | the
=   même | same

fr  Un jour, Madame Dubois décide d'attacher un mot au guidon.
en  One day, Madame Dubois decides to attach a note to the handlebar.
?   d' | elision-before-vowel
    «de» runs into «attacher», a verb starting with a vowel, and contracts
    the same way it did before «un» a few lines back. The apostrophe is the
    only trace left of the missing «e».
=   d' | of

fr  Sur le vélo, il y a un mot : « Ce vélo est à qui ? »
en  On the bicycle, there's a note: "Whose bike is this?"
?   il y a | il-y-a-impersonal
    None of the three words means anything on its own here — together they
    just mean "there is." It's how the note gets introduced into the story,
    tied to the bicycle rather than to any person.
=   le | the
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   est | is
=   à | to

fr  Pendant trois jours, il n'y a pas de réponse.
en  For three days, there's no reply.
?   il n'y a pas | il-y-a-impersonal
    The same fixed phrase as before, now negated: «il n'y a pas» means "there
    isn't." The three days of silence are what makes the eventual reply land
    as a surprise.
?   de | de-after-negation
    A plain sentence would say «il y a une réponse». Once it's negated, the
    article «une» shrinks down to bare «de» — French stops marking the amount
    once the thing doesn't exist.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   pas | not-2
=   de | any

fr  Madame Dubois la lit à voix haute, devant tous les voisins.
en  Madame Dubois reads it aloud, in front of all the neighbours.
?   la | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «la» stands for the reply, «réponse», which is feminine — that's why it
    isn't «le» — and it moves in front of «lit» rather than sitting after it,
    the way English does with "reads it."
=   la | it
=   à | at
=   les | the

en  The reply is simple: "It's my bike."

fr  « Je le déplace chaque jour pour faire croire à ma femme que je fais du sport. »
en  "I move it every day to make my wife believe I'm exercising."
?   le | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «le» stands for the bicycle again and sits in front of «déplace», the
    same placement rule as every other object pronoun in this story.
?   pour faire croire | preposition-plus-infinitive
    After the preposition «pour», French uses the plain dictionary form of
    the verb, «faire», not an "-ing" form. English would say "to make her
    believe," with "to" doing the same job «pour» does here.
?   fais du sport | faire-expressions
    «faire du sport» is a fixed block meaning to exercise — literally it
    reads "make of the sport," which is nonsense assembled word by word.
    Learn it as one piece, the way you'd learn "faire attention."
=   le | it
=   à | to
=   femme | wife

=   les | the

=   l' | the

fr  Madame Dubois est allée voir le vieil homme pour dire merci.
en  Madame Dubois went to see the old man to say thank you.
?   est allée | passe-compose-etre
    «aller» is one of the small set of verbs that build their past with
    «être» instead of «avoir». Because the subject is Madame Dubois, a woman,
    the participle picks up an «e»: «allée».
?   vieil | adjective-before-vowel
    «vieux» would put two vowel sounds back to back in «vieux homme», so
    French swaps in the special form «vieil» instead — the same move the
    story's very first line made with «l'immeuble».
=   est | is
=   le | the

fr  Depuis ce jour, le vélo jaune reste toujours au même endroit.
en  Since that day, the yellow bicycle always stays in the same spot.
?   depuis | duration-pendant-depuis
    «depuis ce jour» marks the day the habit started, and the habit is still
    going on now — that's what «depuis» signals, unlike «pendant», which
    would mean the habit was already over and done.
=   le | the
=   même | same

fr  Personne ne connaît son adresse, mais tout le monde connaît son vélo.
en  Nobody knows his address, but everyone knows his bicycle.
?   son adresse | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «adresse» is feminine, so you'd expect «sa» — but it starts with a vowel,
    and «sa» becomes «son» there purely for the sound, the same shift the
    rule describes for words like «amie». It still means his address, not
    anyone else's.
=   personne | nobody
=   son | his
=   le | the

fr  Chaque matin, il dit bonjour, et son vélo jaune attend à côté de lui.
en  Every morning, he says hello, and his yellow bicycle waits beside him.
?   à côté de lui | stressed-pronouns
    After a preposition like «à côté de», French needs the standalone
    pronoun «lui», not a form that glues onto a verb. It's the same slot
    «elle» fills after «au-dessus de».
=   il | he
=   dit | says
=   son | his
=   à | at
=   de | of
=   lui | him
