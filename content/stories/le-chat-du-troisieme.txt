fr  Il y a un chat gris dans l'immeuble.
en  There's a grey cat in the building.
?   il y a | il-y-a-impersonal
    «il», «y», and «a» don't mean it, there, and has on their own here —
    together the three words just mean there is. It's how the cat gets
    introduced into the story, the same way you'd start telling someone
    about him.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   l' | the

fr  Personne ne sait à qui est le chat.
en  Nobody knows whose cat he is.
?   Personne ne | negation-other-pairs
    Here «personne» (nobody) is the one doing the not-knowing, so it comes
    first and «ne» follows it, right before the verb. It's the same
    negative word you'll see later in the story, just moved because this
    time it's the subject.
?   à qui est le chat | etre-a-possession
    «être» plus «à» plus a name is how French says who something belongs
    to. «à qui est le chat ?» is literally "to whom is the cat" — the
    everyday way of asking whose cat it is.
=   personne | nobody
=   à | to
=   est | is
=   le | the

fr  Le chat connaît toutes les portes de l'immeuble.
en  The cat knows every door in the building.
?   toutes | adjective-agreement
    «toutes» agrees with «portes», which is feminine and plural, so it
    picks up both endings. Later on you'll see «tout le monde», where
    «tout» stays masculine and singular because it's describing a
    different, singular idea — everyone, not the doors.
?   de l'immeuble | elision-before-vowel
    «le» runs into «immeuble», which starts with a vowel sound, so it
    drops its own vowel and becomes «l'» — the same contraction you'll see
    with «l'escalier» a couple of lines from here.
=   le | the
=   les | the
=   de | of
=   l' | the

fr  Chaque jour, il monte l'escalier et il choisit une porte.
en  Every day, he climbs the stairs and picks a door.
?   l'escalier | elision-before-vowel
    «le» runs into «escalier», which starts with a vowel sound, so it
    drops its own vowel and becomes «l'». You'll see the same move happen
    to «que» a few lines further on.
=   il | he
=   l' | the

fr  Au premier étage, il mange chez Monsieur Duval.
en  On the first floor, he eats at Monsieur Duval's.
?   chez Monsieur Duval | chez
    «chez» plus a person's name means at that person's home — there's no
    separate French word for "someone's place" the way English just says
    "at Duval's." It always needs «chez» in front.
=   il | he

fr  Là, tout le monde l'appelle Gris.
en  There, everyone calls him Gris.
?   l'appelle | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «l'» stands for the cat and sits right in front of «appelle», the verb
    that's actually doing the calling. English puts it after — calls him
    — but French always moves the pronoun to the left of the verb it
    belongs to.
?   tout le monde | adjective-agreement
    «tout» stays masculine and singular here because «tout le monde» —
    everyone — is a fixed phrase, not a case of agreement changing shape.
    Compare «toutes les portes» a moment ago, where «toutes» really does
    agree, feminine and plural, with «portes».
=   le | the
=   l' | him

=   il | he
=   le | the

fr  Léo habite au troisième étage, juste au-dessus.
en  Léo lives on the third floor, just above.

fr  L'après-midi, le chat dort aussi devant sa fenêtre à lui.
en  In the afternoon, the cat sleeps in front of his own window too.
?   sa ... lui | stressed-pronouns
    «sa fenêtre» already means his window, so adding «à lui» afterwards is
    pure emphasis — his own, not the neighbours'. That extra «lui» is the
    standalone pronoun French adds to underline whose something is.
?   sa fenêtre | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «sa» matches «fenêtre», a feminine word, even though «le chat» is
    «il», grammatically masculine. The possessive always tracks the thing
    owned, never the owner.
=   l' | the
=   le | the
=   sa | his
=   à | to
=   lui | him

fr  Léo lui donne un peu de lait dans une vieille tasse.
en  Léo gives him a little milk in an old cup.
?   lui donne | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «lui» stands for the cat and sits right in front of «donne», the verb
    it belongs to — to him, moved to the left the way object pronouns
    always are in French.
?   un peu de lait | quantity-de
    «un peu» already says how much — a little — so no article follows
    «de». Compare it to «du lait» with no quantity word at all, which
    would just mean some milk with no sense of how much.
?   vieille | adjective-agreement
    «vieille» is the feminine form, matching «tasse», a feminine word. Say
    «vieux tasse» out loud and it clashes — the ending has to change with
    the noun it's describing.
=   lui | to-him
=   de | of

fr  Il l'appelle Nuage, parce qu'il est gris comme un nuage.
en  He calls him Cloud, because he's grey like a cloud.
?   qu'il | elision-before-vowel
    «que» meets «il», another word starting with a vowel sound, and
    contracts the same way «le» did with «escalier» earlier. Say «parce
    que il» out loud and you'll hear why it can't stay whole.
=   il | he
=   l' | him
=   qu' | that
=   est | is

fr  Un jour, Léo a décidé de trouver à qui est le chat.
en  One day, Léo decided to find out whose cat it was.
?   de trouver | preposition-plus-infinitive
    «décider» needs «de» before whatever comes next, and what follows is
    the plain form of the verb, «trouver», never an "-ing" form. English
    says decided to find; French just glues «de» and the infinitive
    straight onto «décidé».
?   à qui est le chat | etre-a-possession
    Same construction as earlier in the story — «être» plus «à» plus who
    something belongs to. Léo isn't hunting for a person called "the
    owner"; he's trying to find out who the cat is «à».
=   de | of
=   à | to
=   est | is
=   le | the

fr  Il a frappé à toutes les portes de l'immeuble.
en  He knocked on every door in the building.
?   frappé à | a-for-places
    «frapper» always comes with «à» attached to it — you don't just knock
    a door in French, you knock at it. English drops the preposition
    entirely, but French verbs like this one keep theirs no matter what.
?   toutes | adjective-agreement
    Same agreement as the first time this phrase showed up. «toutes»
    matches «portes», feminine and plural, so it stays «toutes», not
    «tous» — the ending doesn't change no matter how many doors he's
    already tried.
=   il | he
=   à | at
=   les | the
=   de | of
=   l' | the

fr  « C'est votre chat ? » a-t-il demandé à chaque voisin.
en  "Is this your cat?" he asked each neighbour.
?   a-t-il | euphonic-t-inversion
    Flipping «il a» round to ask a question would give «a-il», two vowels
    crashing into each other. French slides in a meaningless «t» just to
    make the question sayable, the same fix you'd see with any «il» or
    «elle» question in this tense.
=   est | is
=   à | to
=   il | he

fr  « Non, a répondu Monsieur Duval, mais je lui donne à manger. »
en  "No," Monsieur Duval replied, "but I feed him."
?   lui donne | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «lui» stands for the cat and again sits before the verb it belongs to,
    «donne» — the same leftward move as «l'appelle» earlier in the story.
    Here it means to him rather than replacing the cat directly the way
    «l'» did, but the rule about where the pronoun goes doesn't change.
?   à manger | preposition-plus-infinitive
    «donner à manger» means "to feed" — literally "to give to eat". «à»
    here is followed by the plain infinitive «manger», the same pattern as
    «de trouver» earlier, just with a different preposition glued to a
    different verb.
=   lui | to-him
=   à | to

fr  « Non, a répondu la voisine du deuxième, mais il dort chez moi le samedi. »
en  "No," the neighbour on the second floor replied, "but he sleeps at my place on Saturdays."
?   le samedi | definite-article-days
    A bare «samedi» would mean this coming Saturday. Adding «le» turns it
    into a routine — every Saturday, as a rule — which is exactly what the
    neighbour means: a regular thing, not a one-off.
=   la | the
=   il | he
=   le | the

fr  Enfin, Léo a compris : le chat n'est à personne.
en  Finally, Léo understood: the cat doesn't belong to anyone.
?   n' ... personne | negation-other-pairs
    This time «personne» comes after the verb instead of before it,
    because it's the thing being denied — the cat belongs to nobody —
    rather than the one doing the not-knowing, like back in the second
    line. «ne» still wraps around the verb either way.
?   est à personne | etre-a-possession
    «être» plus «à» is how French says something belongs to someone —
    here it's just negated. «à personne» is "belongs to nobody", the same
    shape as «c'est à moi» said the ordinary, positive way.
=   le | the
=   est | is
=   à | to
=   personne | nobody

?   il est à tout l'immeuble | etre-a-possession
    «est à» is the same "belongs to" construction as the rest of the
    story, just aimed at the whole building this time instead of one
    person.
=   qu' | that

fr  Ce soir-là, Léo a laissé sa fenêtre ouverte.
en  That evening, Léo left his window open.
?   sa fenêtre | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    «sa» agrees with «fenêtre», a feminine word, even though the window
    belongs to Léo, a boy. French possessives track the gender of the
    thing owned, not the owner — say «son fenêtre» and it would sound
    wrong before you could explain why.
?   fenêtre ouverte | adjective-position-default
    «ouverte» comes after «fenêtre», which is where most French adjectives
    sit. It isn't on the short list of common words — like «vieux» or
    «petit» — that jump in front of the noun instead.
?   ouverte | adjective-agreement
    «ouverte» adds an «e» to match «fenêtre», feminine. Léo isn't just
    leaving the window open in some generic sense — the word itself has
    to agree with what's open.
=   sa | his

fr  Le chat gris est entré, et il a choisi, comme toujours, la meilleure place.
en  The grey cat came in, and picked, as always, the best spot.
?   est entré | passe-compose-etre
    «entrer» is one of the small set of verbs, like «rester» or
    «arriver», that build their past with «être» instead of «avoir». The
    cat is a single «il», so the participle stays plain «entré» with no
    extra letter.
?   la meilleure place | superlative-le-plus
    «meilleure» is the irregular superlative of «bon» — it means "best",
    not "more good". French skips «plus» entirely for this one word and
    swaps in a different form instead, the way English swaps "good" for
    "best".
=   le | the
=   est | is
=   il | he
=   la | the

fr  Il s'est endormi sur le lit de Léo, et personne n'est venu le chercher.
en  He fell asleep on Léo's bed, and nobody came to fetch him.
?   s'est endormi | reflexive-verbs
    «s'endormir» means to fall asleep, and verbs like this one, where the
    subject is also the target of the action, always build their past
    tense with «être», never «avoir». That's why it's «s'est endormi»,
    not «s'a endormi», even though most verbs in this tense use «avoir».
?   le chercher | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «le» stands for the cat and this time sits in front of «chercher»,
    the infinitive it belongs to, not the conjugated verb «est venu». The
    pronoun always attaches to the verb it's actually the object of,
    wherever that verb sits in the sentence.
=   il | he
=   s' | himself
=   est | is
=   le | the
=   de | of
=   personne | nobody
