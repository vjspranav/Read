fr  Ce mardi matin, il fait encore nuit quand Mamie me réveille.
en  This Tuesday morning, it's still dark when Mamie wakes me up.
?   il fait encore nuit | weather-il
    Nothing is actually "making" the night — «il» is just a placeholder
    subject French sentences need even when nobody is doing anything, the
    same empty «il» you get with «il pleut» or «il est sept heures».
=   il | it

fr  « Debout, dit-elle, j'ai besoin de toi au marché. »
en  "Up you get," she says, "I need you at the market."
?   besoin de | verb-plus-de-fixed
    «avoir besoin de» is glued together as one unit meaning "to need." The
    «de» isn't "of" here and nothing can slide between «besoin» and «de» —
    treat the whole phrase as one block.
=   de | of

fr  Je n'ai pas envie de me lever, mais je la suis dans l'escalier.
en  I don't feel like getting up, but I follow her down the stairs.
?   me lever | reflexive-verbs
    «lever» means to lift something; «se lever» means to get yourself up
    out of bed. The «me» shows the action lands back on the subject — you
    lift yourself.
?   la suis | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «la» stands for Mamie and sits right before «suis», the verb it
    belongs to. Careful: this is «suivre», to follow — not «être», to be —
    so «je la suis» means "I follow her," not "I am her."
=   pas | not-2
=   de | of
=   la | her
=   l' | the

fr  Dehors, il fait très froid, et mes mains sont déjà gelées.
en  Outside, it's very cold, and my hands are already frozen.
?   il fait très froid | faire-expressions
    French reaches for «faire» — literally "to make" — for weather, where
    English uses "to be." «il fait froid» is a fixed block; nobody
    translates «fait» on its own here.
?   gelées | adjective-agreement
    «gelées» picks up an «e» and an «s» to match «mains», which is
    feminine and plural. You can't hear the difference when you say it,
    but it has to be there in writing.
=   il | it

fr  Elle vient ici le mardi depuis quarante ans, même quand il pleut.
en  She's come here every Tuesday for forty years, even when it rains.
?   le mardi | definite-article-days
    A bare «mardi» would mean one particular Tuesday. Adding «le» turns it
    into a routine — every Tuesday, as a rule — which is exactly the habit
    this line is describing.
?   depuis quarante ans | duration-pendant-depuis
    The habit started forty years ago and is still going now, so French
    uses «depuis», not «pendant». If it had already stopped, the sentence
    would need «pendant» instead.

=   même | even
=   il | it

fr  Mamie n'a pas besoin de demander : elle sait déjà ce qu'il faut.
en  Mamie doesn't need to ask: she already knows what she needs.
?   besoin de demander | verb-plus-de-fixed
    Same fixed pair as before — «avoir besoin de» — now with an infinitive

    «il faut» is another job for the empty «il»: nothing "needs" anything

fr  Elle compte ses pièces, mais il n'y a pas assez d'argent pour les tomates.
en  She counts her coins, but there isn't enough money for the tomatoes.
?   il ... a | il-y-a-impersonal
    «il y a» is one fixed block meaning "there is." The negation slides
    its «n'» in between «il» and «y», which looks like it breaks the
    phrase apart, but the three words are still doing their usual job
    together.
?   assez d'argent | quantity-de
    «assez» already says how much — enough — so no article follows «de».
    Compare «de l'argent» with no quantity word, which would just mean
    some money with no amount implied.

en  I look at Mamie, standing in the cold, and I don't say anything more.
?   debout dans le froid | bare-descriptive-phrase

    «plus» here means not any more, not more of it — I used to say

fr  Un vieil homme s'approche lentement, avec une canne.
en  An old man approaches slowly, with a cane.
?   vieil homme | adjective-before-vowel
    «vieux» has a special form just for this spot — right before a
    masculine noun starting with a vowel sound. «vieil homme» is easier to
    say than «vieux homme» would be.
?   s'approche | reflexive-verbs
    «approcher» can mean to bring something closer; «s'approcher» means to
    come closer yourself. The «s'» sends the action back onto the subject,
    the old man himself.

    «de» runs into «habitude», which starts with a vowel sound even though

fr  « Oui, mais juste pour moi maintenant », répond-il, triste.
en  "Yes, but just for me now," he replies sadly.
?   pour moi | stressed-pronouns
    After a preposition like «pour», French needs the standalone pronoun
    «moi», not a form that hides in front of a verb. The little pronouns
    like «me» can't stand alone like this.

    Cities always take «à» — you don't just go "to Lyon" in French, you go
