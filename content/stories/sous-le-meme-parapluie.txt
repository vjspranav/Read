fr  Léa habite dans une petite rue, à côté d'une boulangerie.
en  Léa lives on a small street, next to a bakery.
?   d' | elision-before-vowel
    «de» would run straight into «une», two vowel sounds with nothing between
    them, so it drops its own vowel and becomes «d'». You'll see the same move
    happen to «le» and «la» later in the story.
=   à | at
=   d' | of

fr  Chaque matin, elle achète du pain et des croissants avant le travail.
en  Every morning, she buys bread and croissants before work.
?   du | partitive-article
    Léa isn't buying one specific, whole loaf — «pain» is masculine, so «de»
    plus «le» contracts to «du», and together they mean some bread, an amount
    with no number attached.
?   des | partitive-article
    Same idea for the croissants: an unspecified number of them, so the
    plural partitive «des» does the job. English just says croissants and
    leaves the amount implied.
=   le | the

fr  Elle attend le bus devant la boulangerie.
en  She waits for the bus in front of the bakery.
=   le | the
=   la | the

fr  Il y a toujours un homme au même arrêt, mais elle ne connaît pas son nom.
en  There's always a man at the same stop, but she doesn't know his name.
?   il y a | il-y-a-impersonal
    Nothing here literally means it, there, or has on its own — the three
    words work as one fixed phrase for there is. It's how the man at the stop
    gets introduced into the story.
=   il | it
=   y | there
=   même | same
=   pas | not-2
=   son | his

fr  Un mardi, il pleuvait depuis le matin.
en  One Tuesday, it had been raining since morning.
?   depuis | duration-pendant-depuis
    «depuis» plus this imparfait form, «pleuvait», says the rain had already
    been going on before this moment and was still happening then — not one
    downpour, but weather that was already the backdrop.
=   il | it
=   le | the

fr  Léa a couru jusqu'à l'arrêt de bus.
en  Léa ran to the bus stop.
?   a couru | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    The line before this one set the scene with «pleuvait» — imparfait,
    ongoing. This verb switches to passé composé because running to the stop
    is a single, finished action: she started, she arrived, done.
?   jusqu'à | jusqua
    «à l'arrêt» alone would just name where she ended up. «jusqu'à» adds the
    whole distance she covered getting there — she ran the entire way.
=   à | to
=   l' | the
=   de | of

fr  Elle a cherché son parapluie dans son sac, mais il n'était pas là.
en  She looked for her umbrella in her bag, but it wasn't there.
?   n'était pas | imparfait-vs-passe-compose
    Searching her bag, «a cherché», is the event; the umbrella simply not
    being there is the state she found. That's why the two verbs sit in
    different tenses in the same sentence.
?   son sac | possessive-agrees-with-noun
    Two lines, two different things called «son» — «son parapluie» and now
    «son sac» — because both «parapluie» and «sac» are masculine words, not
    because two different people own them. It's always Léa's.
=   son | his
=   il | it
=   était | was
=   pas | not-2

fr  Elle l'a oublié sur la table de la cuisine.
en  She'd left it on the kitchen table.
?   l'a | object-pronoun-before-verb
    «l'» stands for the umbrella and sits right in front of «a», the helping
    verb. English puts it after the verb — left it — but French moves it left
    and keeps it there.
=   l' | it
=   la | the
=   de | of

fr  Le bus n'était pas encore là.
en  The bus wasn't there yet.
=   le | the
=   était | was
=   pas | not-2

fr  L'homme a levé son parapluie noir au-dessus d'elle.
en  The man raised his black umbrella above her.
?   d' | elision-before-vowel
    «de» meets «elle», another vowel start, and contracts the same way it did
    in the first line of the story — this time after the compound preposition
    «au-dessus», above.
?   au-dessus d'elle | stressed-pronouns
    After a preposition like «au-dessus de», French needs the standalone
    pronoun «elle», not a form that hides in front of a verb. It's the same
    slot «lui» fills after «sans» or «chez».
=   l' | the
=   son | his
=   d' | of

fr  « Vous n'avez pas de parapluie ? » a-t-il demandé.
en  "You don't have an umbrella?" he asked.
?   pas de parapluie | de-after-negation
    Asked as a plain fact, this would be «un parapluie». Once «ne … pas» is
    in the sentence, the article shrinks to bare «de» — the same drop you'd
    see in a statement.
?   a-t-il | euphonic-t-inversion
    Flipping «il a» to make a question would give «a-il», two vowels
    colliding. French drops in a «t» that carries no meaning at all — it's
    only there to make the question sayable.
=   pas | not-2
=   de | any
=   il | he

fr  « Non, je l'ai oublié », a répondu Léa, et elle a ri.
en  "No, I forgot it," Léa replied, and she laughed.
?   l'ai | elision-before-vowel
    «le», standing for the umbrella, meets «ai» and loses its vowel the same
    way «de» and «que» do elsewhere in the story. Say «le ai» out loud and
    you'll hear why it can't stay whole.
=   l' | it

fr  Ils sont restés sous le parapluie jusqu'à l'arrivée du bus.
en  They stayed under the umbrella until the bus arrived.
?   sont restés | passe-compose-etre
    «rester» is one of the small set of verbs that build their past with
    «être» instead of «avoir». Because the subject is «ils», the participle
    picks up an «s»: «restés», the way an adjective would.
?   jusqu'à | jusqua
    Same word, same job as the first time it appeared: not just where they
    end up, but the whole stretch of time they stood there, right up to the
    bus turning up.
=   le | the
=   à | to
=   l' | the

fr  Dans le bus, ils ont parlé pour la première fois.
en  On the bus, they talked for the first time.
?   première | adjective-agreement
    «fois» is feminine, so the ordinal «premier» takes its feminine form,
    «première», with the extra «e». Say «premier fois» out loud in French and
    it sounds wrong before you can explain why.
=   le | the
=   la | the

fr  Elle a appris qu'il s'appelle Thomas et qu'il travaille près de chez elle.
en  She learned his name was Thomas, and that he works near her place.
?   qu' | reported-speech-que | 1
    English would happily drop that here: she learned he's called Thomas.
    French can't; «que» has to introduce what she learned, and it shortens to
    «qu'» because «il» starts with a vowel.
?   chez elle | chez
    «chez elle» means at her place, near where she lives — there's no French
    word that just means home the way English has one, so «chez» plus the
    person does that job instead.
=   qu' | that
=   il | he
=   s' | himself
=   de | of

fr  Depuis ce jour, Léa n'oublie plus son parapluie.
en  Since that day, Léa never forgets her umbrella.
?   depuis | duration-pendant-depuis
    That Tuesday marked a starting point, and the habit is still going now,
    so it's «depuis», not «pendant». If the habit had already ended, French
    would switch to «pendant ce jour-là».
?   n'oublie plus | negation-other-pairs
    «plus» here means not any more, not more — she used to forget it and now
    she doesn't. Read the sentence with a silent «s» on «plus» and that's the
    sense you want.
=   plus | no-more
=   son | his

fr  Elle en apporte même un deuxième, pour Thomas.
en  She even brings a second one, for Thomas.
?   en apporte | pronoun-en
    «en» stands in for parapluies without naming it again — a second one of
    them. Leave it out and «elle apporte même un deuxième» is missing a
    piece; French won't let the sentence stand without it.
=   en | of-them
=   même | even

fr  Il ne pleut pas toujours, mais elle l'apporte quand même.
en  It doesn't always rain, but she brings it anyway.
?   il | weather-il
    Same empty «il» as the rain earlier in the story — nothing is doing the
    raining, the pronoun is just a placeholder the sentence needs to have a
    subject at all.
?   ne ... pas | negation-wraps-verb
    «pleut» is the only verb in the sentence, so that's what the negation
    wraps — no helping verb here to argue about, unlike the compound tenses
    earlier in the story.
=   il | it
=   pas | not-2
=   l' | it
=   même | even

fr  Un matin, Thomas arrive avec deux cafés.
en  One morning, Thomas arrives with two coffees.

fr  « Pour vous, dit-il, avec ou sans pluie. »
en  "For you," he says, "rain or not."

fr  Maintenant, ils attendent le bus ensemble, chaque matin.
en  Now, they wait for the bus together, every morning.
=   le | the

fr  Et Léa ne rentre plus seule quand il pleut.
en  And Léa doesn't walk home alone anymore when it rains.
?   ne rentre plus | negation-other-pairs
    The same not any more as before, now about walking home alone rather
    than forgetting an umbrella — the routine that changed is different, but
    the grammar doing the work is identical.
?   seule | adjective-agreement
    «seul» adds an «e» to agree with Léa, who's «elle», not «il». Drop the
    «e» and the sentence would technically describe a man walking home
    alone.
=   plus | no-more
=   il | it
